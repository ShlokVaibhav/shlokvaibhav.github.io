# What was Hume's profession?

Hume was not a scientist in terms of rigour or method, his aim was inquiry of human nature where he shatters rationality and the practical applications of this nature - history, politics, society and economy. His method were common-sensical and proto-bayesian but not scientific. That's why after finishing his treatise at my age, he moves to its application - history, politics and other ventures. There is a continuity amidst the rupture - *he sharpens his knives in the treatise*, then proceeds to the surgery of other domains of his interest.

# What are his core beliefs and methods

He is sceptic who knows one cannot live full-time sceptic, for middle path is his. His love is the love of literary fame, his aim to infuse inspiration and hope into the masses with his philosophy of moderation. Unlike the continental dogmatist (That's what Kant said), Hume never doubts the existence of a permanent human nature or that of other humans, he is sceptic of the claim that there is something God-like or demon-like inside the man, there's none as per him. *For Hume, strict proof plays no part in human life outside mathematics*

He find the humanity amusing and inspiring but not to the extent universe becomes anthropocentric. (Sorry Parfit!, Hume is fond of humans even when long-termism was not tenable). 
Being an empiricist that he is, he grounds his investigation of human nature and the ramifications of human nature (Society, commerce, politics) in his great grasp over history and classical texts rather than the motivated abstractions of Locke whom he characteristically humiliates.

# Hume and causality

I learnt that the greatest thinker on causality ever is our great infidel. He gives a simple case study of two humans who have exactly the same information about sequence of certain events, except that one has been exposed to the same information multiple times and hence has developed a notion of "Causality". Thence, Hume concludes causality is a property of human mind and not a fact about the nature of reality. 

My own speculation is that causality is not just another property of human mind - it is a crucial property that correlates with evolutionary advantage (It would be an irony if i were to say it is a property that confers evolutionary advantage!). Seen this way, it also conciles with Hume's claim that animals are endowed more reason than human credit them for - if it is an evolutionary advantage, animals will have some degree of notion of causality in them!

# Hume and the religion

Admittedly this is section we had all been waiting for. In this summary yet nothing much to be written here, for whatever has been written above is iconoclastic enough (And the fact that it shatters his enemies without even naming them - quite magnanimous). 
There's a passing reference at time to Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, published few years after his death, which i haven't been exposed to before. 

There's something new - Apparently theists used to claim since "Space and Time" is a paradoxical phenomenon which everyone readily accepts, you cannot claim the paradoxical nature of revealed texts as a claim against religion either. Hume shatters this in his signature move of deconstructing "Space and Time" as merely a presentation about the ordering of things with respect to each other in our brain. 

# Hume's relevance in our times

Few pages are devoted to remark on the striking relevance and the presence of Hume in modern philosophy and science (That such a statement is being made is in itself a sufficient rebuttal to the claim that Kant settled scores with Hume after Hume woke Kant from his sweet dogmatic slumber). 

Since the reader is already with the zeitgeist of our times, let me just make passing references to the modern themes that trace their lineage to our childless infidel: 

1. Logical positivism
2. The forking of British empiricism from continental dogmatism 
3. The problem of induction and non-universality of scientific laws
4. Tracing human reason and logic to psychology and passions

"The mere fact that a scientific law is claimed to be universal precludes the possibility of it being logically derived from observations"

Hume's fork:

Given any thought you're entertaining, ask:

1. Does this thought concern matters of fact — if so, does it rest on observation and experience?
2. Does it concern relation between ideas — for example in mathematics or logic?

If the answer to both is no, the thought is not worth having.
